\section{Jumping attempts examples}
In order to test our solutions and ideas, we used recordings of ski jumping attempts from the Four Hills Tournament 2025/2026 (the most recent one) from the two events:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Obersdorf (GER) - M - Large Hill Qualis - Viessmann FIS Ski Jumping World Cup (28.12.2025)
    \item Garmisch (GER) - M - Large Hill Qualis - Viessmann FIS Ski Jumping World Cup (31.12.2025)
\end{enumerate}

By processing the raw live streams, we extracted \textbf{45 individual jumping clips} from the Oberstdorf event and \textbf{67 clips from Garmisch-Partenkirchen}. All videos were captured at a resolution of 1920$\times$1080 (Full HD). This resulted in a total of 112 unique jumping attempts for analysis.

\section{Transformations between frames}
The goal of this stage is to estimate the \textbf{global camera motion} inside each shot, so that every detected jumper position can be expressed in a common reference system (we use the \textbf{first frame of the shot} as reference). Since shot boundaries introduce discontinuities (different viewpoint/zoom), we first perform \textbf{shot-change detection} and process each shot independently. \textit{(Implementation: shot segmentation and per-shot processing loop.)}

\subsection{Motion estimation: ECC vs feature-based RANSAC}
We implemented two alternative strategies to estimate the transformation between consecutive frames:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ECC (intensity-based alignment).} We use \texttt{cv2.findTransformECC} to align the current frame to the previous one under a chosen warp model. To reduce the influence of the moving athlete, a \textbf{binary mask} is applied and computed as the intersection of the two frame masks (valid background pixels only). In practice, ECC often fails to converge or becomes unstable when the sequence contains motion blur, illumination changes, low texture regions, or large inter-frame motion. This results in jitter and drift in the accumulated trajectory.

    \item \textbf{SIFT + RANSAC (feature-based).} We detect SIFT keypoints/descriptors on the masked images, match them with a ratio test, and estimate the motion with RANSAC. This approach is robust to outliers and partial occlusions, and it provides a natural confidence score (e.g., inlier ratio) that we store as the edge weight. Empirically, this method is both more stable and faster on our sequences than ECC.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Choosing the warp model: homography vs affine vs partial affine}
A key design choice is the \textbf{parametric motion model}. In the hierarchy of 2D transformations, a homography (projective transform) is the most general model for mapping one plane to another, while affine/similarity models are more constrained and typically more stable in the presence of noise.

\paragraph{Homography (projective, 8 DoF).}
We first tested a full homography. While theoretically powerful, it is \textbf{not stable} in our scenario: ski-jumping broadcast videos usually contain \textbf{parallax} (scene points at different depths), slight zoom changes, motion blur, and repeated textures (snow, stands). In these conditions, a projective model can overfit mismatches, producing unrealistic perspective/shear artifacts that amplify drift when composed over time.

\paragraph{Affine (6 DoF).}
Switching to an affine model already improves stability, because it removes projective distortions and reduces overfitting. However, full affine still allows shear, which can be triggered by residual mismatches and gradually distort the reference frame over long compositions.

\paragraph{Partial affine / similarity (4 DoF).}
Our final choice is \textbf{affine partial 2D} (OpenCV \texttt{estimateAffinePartial2D}), which corresponds to a similarity-like transform (translation + rotation + isotropic scale, without shear). This is a good compromise: it captures the dominant camera motion while being sufficiently constrained to reduce drift and deformation.

\section{How we propagate transformations through time}
Once pairwise transformations are available, the remaining question is how to obtain, for each frame $k$, a transform $H_{k\rightarrow 0}$ mapping points from frame $k$ to the reference frame $0$ of the current shot.

\paragraph{Cumulative composition (final choice).}
For each shot we set:
\[
H_{0\rightarrow 0} = I,\qquad
H_{k\rightarrow 0} = H_{k\rightarrow k-1}\,H_{k-1\rightarrow 0}.
\]
Given a detected jumper point $p_k = (x_k,y_k)$, we map it to the reference as
\[
\tilde p_k \sim H_{k\rightarrow 0}\,[x_k\; y_k\; 1]^T.
\]
This is the approach used to generate the final trajectories (per-shot), and it corresponds to the \texttt{cumulative} branch in our trajectory code.

\paragraph{Graph-based global estimation (tested, not selected).}
We also experimented with a graph formulation: each frame is a node, and each estimated motion constraint is an edge $(i,j)$ with a warp $W_{ij}$ (mapping $i\rightarrow j$) and a confidence weight. We solve for all per-frame transforms $\{H_i\}$ by minimizing a weighted consistency error of the form
\[
\sum_{(i,j)\in E} w_{ij}\,\lVert H_i - H_j W_{ij}\rVert^2,
\]
plus a gauge constraint $H_{\text{ref}} = I$ to fix the reference frame. The system is assembled as a sparse least-squares problem and solved with LSQR; we also included an IRLS-style robust reweighting step to downweight inconsistent edges.

In theory, adding longer edges (e.g., $k\rightarrow k-2$, $k\rightarrow k-3$) should create loops and reduce drift. In practice, the result was not competitive on our data. The final graph trajectories showed more artifacts than the simple cumulative baseline, therefore we kept the cumulative approach.

\section{Fitting lines and homography problem}\label{sec:homography}
After aligning the trajectory to a common reference frame (via inter-frame \emph{partial affine 2D}
stabilization), we investigated a plane-rectification approach based on vanishing geometry.
In principle, estimating an affine and then metric rectification of the motion plane requires detecting
(at least) two dominant directions on the plane, i.e., two sets of real-world parallel lines, ideally
with one set perpendicular to the other.

\medskip

In practice, this requirement could not be satisfied reliably in our video shots.
Across different views, the only geometric structure that remains consistently visible and stable is
given by the parallel lines of the ski track itself.
Other potentially useful cues (e.g., a second independent family of parallels, right angles, or
repeatable landmarks) are either absent, too noisy, or not consistently observable due to viewpoint
changes, motion blur, and occlusions.
As a consequence, we cannot robustly estimate the vanishing line of the plane, nor enforce metric
constraints (such as orthogonality) needed to remove perspective in a principled way.

\subsection{Fitting lines}
We therefore limit the line fitting process to the track boundaries, which provide a single dominant
direction in the image.
While these lines can be fitted reliably by least squares (or robust fitting) in each shot, a single
family of parallels is insufficient to fully determine the plane rectification.
The figures below show the fitted track lines for the inrun and landing frames, highlighting both the
availability of one consistent direction and the lack of additional stable geometric constraints.

\subsection{Inrun frame challenges}
The primary challenge in this phase is the significant variability between different ski jumping hills and the broadcasting techniques used. While the inrun tracks provide a consistent pair of parallel lines across all events, the second pair of reference lines (necessary for complete rectification) varies significantly depending on the specific hill architecture.

Furthermore, the method of filming poses a major obstacle. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:frame_variation_1} and Figure \ref{fig:frame_variation_2}, even within the same event, the framing can change drastically. Some jumpers are recorded with a wide view covering the entire inrun, while others are captured with a close-up tracking shot. These distinct perspectives cannot be processed using a single, generalized algorithm.

Due to these inconsistencies, we were unable to develop a universal algorithm capable of automatically fitting two pairs of parallel lines for every case. Each specific camera angle and hill configuration would require a tailored approach.

\subsection{Landing frame challenges}
In the landing frame, the main limitation is the lack of a second pair of reliable reference lines. While the K-point and Hill Size (HS) lines form one parallel pair, the side boundaries of the landing area (bands) or other visible markings are rarely strictly perpendicular to them or consistently visible. Consequently, we were only able to reliably extract the K and HS lines in these frames, which is insufficient for full metric rectification without additional constraints.

\section{Final pipeline choice}
To summarize, the final configuration used in the project is:
\begin{itemize}
    \item per-shot processing (shot-change detection and reset of the reference);
    \item background-only motion estimation via masking;
    \item SIFT matching + RANSAC estimation;
    \item \textbf{partial affine 2D} motion model;
    \item \textbf{cumulative} composition to obtain $H_{k\rightarrow 0}$ and map the jumper trajectory.
\end{itemize}
