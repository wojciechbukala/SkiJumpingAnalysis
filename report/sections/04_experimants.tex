\section{Jumping attempts examples}
In order to test our solutions and ideas, we used recordings of ski jumping attempts from the Four Hills Tournament 2025/2026 (the most recent one) from the two events:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Obersdorf (GER) - M - Large Hill Qualis - Viessmann FIS Ski Jumping World Cup (28.12.2025)
    \item Garmisch (GER) - M - Large Hill Qualis - Viessmann FIS Ski Jumping World Cup (31.12.2025)
\end{enumerate}

By processing the raw live streams, we extracted \textbf{45 individual jumping clips} from the Oberstdorf event and \textbf{67 clips from Garmisch-Partenkirchen}. All videos were captured at a resolution of 1920$\times$1080 (Full HD). This resulted in a total of 112 unique jumping attempts for analysis.

\section{Transformations between frames}
The goal of this stage is to estimate the \textbf{global camera motion} inside the selected camera interval, so that every detected jumper position can be expressed in a common reference system. The reference is a frame selected by the user. Since shot boundaries introduce discontinuities (different viewpoint/zoom), we first perform \textbf{shot-change detection} and process the selected interval independently. \textit{(Implementation: shot segmentation and selected-interval processing loop.)}

\subsection{Motion estimation: ECC vs feature-based RANSAC}
We implemented two alternative strategies to estimate the transformation between consecutive frames:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ECC (intensity-based alignment).} We use \texttt{cv2.findTransformECC} to align the current frame to the previous one under a chosen warp model. To reduce the influence of the moving athlete, a \textbf{binary mask} is applied and computed as the intersection of the two frame masks (valid background pixels only). In practice, ECC often fails to converge or becomes unstable when the sequence contains motion blur, illumination changes, low texture regions, or large inter-frame motion. This results in jitter and drift in the accumulated trajectory.

    \item \textbf{SIFT + RANSAC (feature-based).} We detect SIFT keypoints/descriptors on the masked images, match them with a ratio test, and estimate the motion with RANSAC. This approach is robust to outliers and partial occlusions, and it provides a natural confidence score (e.g., inlier ratio) that we store as the edge weight. Empirically, this method is both more stable and faster on our sequences than ECC.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Choosing the warp model: homography and affine fallback}
A key design choice is the \textbf{parametric motion model}. In the hierarchy of 2D transformations, a homography (projective transform) is the most general model for mapping one plane to another, while affine/similarity models are more constrained and typically more stable in the presence of noise.

\paragraph{Homography (projective, 8 DoF).}
The current implementation first estimates a homography with RANSAC. This is the most flexible model available in the pipeline and can represent the dominant projective camera motion between two consecutive frames.

\paragraph{Affine (6 DoF).}
If the homography score is not reliable, the code tries an affine fallback. If this also fails, the identity transform is used for that frame pair and the corresponding motion score is propagated to mark the affected trajectory points as less reliable.

\end{figure}

\section{How we propagate transformations through time}
Once pairwise transformations are available, the remaining question is how to obtain, for each frame \(k\), a cumulative transform \(H_{k\rightarrow r}\) mapping points from frame \(k\) to the user-selected reference frame \(r\) of the current camera interval.

\paragraph{Cumulative composition (final choice).}
Let \(H_{r\rightarrow r}\) be the identity transform at the reference frame, and let \(H_{k\rightarrow k-1}\) be the pairwise transform from frame \(k\) to the previous frame. For frames after the reference frame, the cumulative composition is:
\[
H_{r\rightarrow r} = I,\qquad
H_{k\rightarrow r} = H_{k-1\rightarrow r}\,H_{k\rightarrow k-1}.
\]
For frames before the reference frame, the implementation composes the inverse pairwise transformations. Given a detected jumper point \(p_k = (x_k,y_k)\), we map it to the reference as
\[
\tilde p_k \sim H_{k\rightarrow r}\,[x_k\; y_k\; 1]^T.
\]
This is the approach used to generate the final trajectory for the selected camera interval.

\paragraph{Graph-based global estimation (tested, not selected).}
We also experimented with a graph formulation. In this formulation, \(E\) is the set of graph edges; each frame is a node; each edge \((i,j)\in E\) stores a warp \(W_{ij}\), mapping frame \(i\) to frame \(j\), and a confidence weight \(w_{ij}\). The unknowns are the per-frame transforms \(\{H_i\}\). We solve for them by minimizing a weighted consistency error of the form
\[
\sum_{(i,j)\in E} w_{ij}\,\lVert H_i - H_j W_{ij}\rVert^2,
\]
plus a gauge constraint \(H_{\text{ref}} = I\) to fix the reference frame. The system is assembled as a sparse least-squares problem and solved with LSQR; we also included an IRLS-style robust reweighting step to downweight inconsistent edges.

In theory, adding longer edges (e.g., $k\rightarrow k-2$, $k\rightarrow k-3$) should create loops and reduce drift. In practice, the result was not competitive on our data. The final graph trajectories showed more artifacts than the simple cumulative baseline, therefore we kept the cumulative approach.

\paragraph{Kalman filtering and reliability selection (selected).} The final trajectory does not use a separate polynomial post-processing step. The current pipeline filters the detected center-of-mass sequence earlier with a constant-velocity Kalman model, using the predicted velocity to reduce frame-to-frame measurement noise while still correcting the state with each valid detection. After mapping points to the selected reference frame, the code propagates the confidence scores of the pairwise motion estimates along the cumulative chain. Points whose cumulative score falls below the configured threshold are marked as less reliable and are excluded from the final plot when reliability-only plotting is enabled. This keeps smoothing tied to the measurement model and avoids an additional smoothing stage on the reconstructed trajectory.

\section{Fitting lines and homography problem}\label{sec:homography}
After aligning the trajectory to a common reference frame, we investigated a plane-rectification approach based on vanishing geometry.
In principle, estimating an affine and then metric rectification of the motion plane requires detecting
(at least) two dominant directions on the plane, i.e., two sets of real-world parallel lines, ideally
with one set perpendicular to the other.

\medskip

In practice, this requirement could not be satisfied reliably in our video shots.
Across different views, the only geometric structure that remains consistently visible and stable is
given by the parallel lines of the ski track itself.
Other potentially useful cues (e.g., a second independent family of parallels, right angles, or
repeatable landmarks) are either absent, too noisy, or not consistently observable due to viewpoint
changes, motion blur, and occlusions.
As a consequence, we cannot robustly estimate the vanishing line of the plane, nor enforce metric
constraints (such as orthogonality) needed to remove perspective in a principled way.

\subsection{Fitting lines}
We therefore limit the line fitting process to the track boundaries, which provide a single dominant
direction in the image.
While these lines can be fitted reliably by least squares (or robust fitting) in each shot, a single
family of parallels is insufficient to fully determine the plane rectification.
The figures below show the fitted track lines for the inrun and landing frames, highlighting both the
availability of one consistent direction and the lack of additional stable geometric constraints.

\subsection{Inrun frame challenges}
The primary challenge in this phase is the significant variability between different ski jumping hills and the broadcasting techniques used. While the inrun tracks provide a consistent pair of parallel lines across all events, the second pair of reference lines (necessary for complete rectification) varies significantly depending on the specific hill architecture.

Furthermore, the method of filming poses a major obstacle. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:frame_variation_1} and Figure \ref{fig:frame_variation_2}, even within the same event, the framing can change drastically. Some jumpers are recorded with a wide view covering the entire inrun, while others are captured with a close-up tracking shot. These distinct perspectives cannot be processed using a single, generalized algorithm.

Due to these inconsistencies, we were unable to develop a universal algorithm capable of automatically fitting two pairs of parallel lines for every case. Each specific camera angle and hill configuration would require a tailored approach.

\subsection{Landing frame challenges}
In the landing frame, the main limitation is the lack of a second pair of reliable reference lines. While the K-point and Hill Size (HS) lines form one parallel pair, the side boundaries of the landing area (bands) or other visible markings are rarely strictly perpendicular to them or consistently visible. Consequently, we were only able to reliably extract the K and HS lines in these frames, which is insufficient for full metric rectification without additional constraints.

\section{Final pipeline choice}
To summarize, the final configuration used in the project is:
\begin{itemize}
    \item selected-camera processing after shot-change detection;
    \item background-only motion estimation via masking;
    \item Kalman-filtered center-of-mass measurements;
    \item SIFT matching + RANSAC estimation;
    \item homography motion model with affine fallback when needed;
    \item reliability filtering through cumulative motion scores;
    \item \textbf{cumulative} composition to obtain \(H_{k\rightarrow r}\) and map the jumper trajectory to the user-selected reference frame \(r\).
\end{itemize}

\section{Implementation attempts and results}
After the image-space trajectory had been stabilized, the main development effort moved to the geometric reconstruction stage. The goal was to combine camera calibration, slope reconstruction, and trajectory lifting into a single pipeline capable of placing the jumper trajectory on a 3D model of the ski-jumping track. This part of the project required several iterations because the final result is very sensitive to the intrinsic matrix \(K\), to the estimated vanishing points, and to the correspondences between the two visible track borders.

\subsection{Calibration sensitivity on the first sequence}
The first reconstruction tests were performed on the reference frame shown in \Cref{fig:impl_video1_reference_frame}. This frame was selected because the track borders are visible enough to annotate the slope geometry and to test the conversion from image points to 3D rays.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{images/new_images/selected_frame1.jpg}
    \caption{Reference frame selected for the first reconstruction experiment.}
    \label{fig:impl_video1_reference_frame}
\end{figure}

The first attempt estimated the full intrinsic matrix \(K\) from the available line geometry. In practice this was not stable enough. When \(K\) is poorly estimated, the inverse projection \(K^{-1}\) produces incorrect camera rays; as a consequence, the reconstructed track can be stretched, tilted, or placed in an unrealistic direction. The failure shown in \Cref{fig:impl_video1_bad_intrinsics_main} should therefore not be interpreted only as an incorrect pixel-size effect. The main issue is that wrong intrinsic parameters, especially focal length and principal point, change the direction of all back-projected rays before the 3D reconstruction is even solved.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{images/new_images/pista1_k_male1.png}
    \caption{First reconstruction of the first sequence with an unstable estimate of \(K\). The track is excessively elongated, which indicates that the back-projected camera rays are not geometrically reliable.}
    \label{fig:impl_video1_bad_intrinsics_main}
\end{figure}

The same failure is visible from a different viewing angle in \Cref{fig:impl_video1_bad_intrinsics_side}. The lateral view makes the problem easier to diagnose: the camera direction and the reconstructed slope orientation are inconsistent with the selected video frame.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{images/new_images/pista1_k_male2.png}
    \caption{Alternative view of the first failed reconstruction. Besides the scale distortion, the estimated camera direction is not consistent with the expected slope geometry.}
    \label{fig:impl_video1_bad_intrinsics_side}

This is a reasonable assumption for broadcast footage when no calibration pattern or sensor metadata is available. With fewer degrees of freedom, the calibration became much more stable and avoided the impossible or numerically degenerate values that often appeared when the whole matrix \(K\) was estimated. The improvement is visible in \Cref{fig:impl_video1_centered_principal_point} and \Cref{fig:impl_video1_centered_principal_point_side}.

\subsection{Correspondence estimation attempts}
In parallel with the calibration experiments, we also tested automatic ways to match points on the left and right track borders. The first idea was to use RANSAC to estimate the dominant correspondence geometry and recover the vanishing point of the cross-track direction. However, the result in \Cref{fig:impl_correspondence_ransac_unconstrained} shows a typical failure case: the estimated vanishing point falls inside the track region, producing correspondences that are not physically meaningful for the reconstructed slope.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{images/new_images/corresp1.png}
    \caption{First automatic correspondence attempt based on RANSAC. The estimated vanishing point falls inside the track region, leading to unreliable left-right matches.}
    \label{fig:impl_correspondence_ransac_unconstrained}
\end{figure}

The second attempt added a geometric constraint to prevent the vanishing point from being accepted in the central forbidden region. This removed some degenerate solutions, but it did not make the method reliable enough. As shown in \Cref{fig:impl_correspondence_ransac_constrained}, the matches can still become unstable because the visible borders are noisy, partially ambiguous, and strongly affected by perspective.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{images/new_images/corresp2.png}
    \caption{Second automatic correspondence attempt with an additional constraint on the vanishing-point location. The constraint reduces one failure mode, but the resulting correspondences are still not robust enough.}
    \label{fig:impl_correspondence_ransac_constrained}
\end{figure}

These experiments guided the final implementation choices. We kept the selected reference frame explicit, constrained the calibration by fixing the principal point at the image center when necessary, inspected the reconstructed trajectory from multiple 3D viewpoints, and avoided relying only on unconstrained RANSAC for border correspondences. The main conclusion is that the 2D detection and stabilization stages are sufficiently stable, while the metric reconstruction is dominated by the quality of the camera calibration and the geometric consistency of the track-border correspondences.
