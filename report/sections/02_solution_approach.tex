\section{Solution}
The proposed pipeline is organized into five main stages: athlete localization, temporal filtering, shot segmentation, camera-motion compensation, and trajectory mapping to a common reference frame.

\medskip

\textbf{Athlete detection (YOLO) and ROI extraction.}  
For each frame, YOLO detects the jumper and provides the bounding box used as the region of interest for segmentation and tracking.

\medskip

\textbf{Pixel-level segmentation (SAM) and center-of-mass measurement.}  
SAM is applied inside the ROI to obtain the jumper mask. The center of mass of the mask is used as the image-space measurement of the jumper position.

\medskip

\textbf{Temporal stabilization (Kalman filter).}  
The raw center-of-mass sequence is filtered with a constant-velocity Kalman filter to reduce jitter and isolated segmentation errors.

\medskip

\textbf{Shot segmentation (HSV cut detection).}  
Camera cuts are detected by comparing consecutive HSV histograms. The video is split into homogeneous shots, which are processed independently because frame alignment is meaningful only within a consistent viewpoint.

\medskip

\textbf{Per-shot motion compensation (feature-based alignment).}  
For each shot, the reference is a frame selected by the user, chosen among the frames where the jumper and the relevant scene geometry are visible. Consecutive-frame motion is estimated on grayscale frames while masking the jumper bounding box and broadcast overlays. In the current implementation, SIFT keypoints are matched with L2 distance and Lowe's ratio test; a homography is then estimated with RANSAC. If the homography estimate is not reliable, the pipeline falls back to an affine model or, when necessary, to the identity transform.

\medskip

\textbf{Cumulative composition to the shot reference.}  
Let \(W_{k\rightarrow k-1}\) be the pairwise transformation mapping coordinates from frame \(k\) to frame \(k-1\). Let \(r\) be the user-selected reference frame, and let \(H_{k\rightarrow r}\) be the cumulative transformation that maps image coordinates from frame \(k\) to the reference frame \(r\). For the reference frame itself, the cumulative transformation is the identity:
\[
H_{r\rightarrow r} = I.
\]
For any other frame, \(H_{k\rightarrow r}\) is obtained by composing the pairwise transformations forward or backward along the selected camera interval. Given the filtered center-of-mass measurement \(p_k = (x_k, y_k)\), we transform it into the reference coordinate system using homogeneous coordinates:
\[
\tilde{p}_k \sim H_{k\rightarrow r}\,[x_k \; y_k \; 1]^T.
\]
This produces a trajectory that is consistent within each shot and compensated for camera motion.

\medskip

\textbf{Final output.}  
The final result is a trajectory for the selected camera interval, expressed in the user-selected reference frame.

\section{From 2D to 3D}
Once the 2D detections have been stabilized, all trajectory points are expressed in the
user-selected reference frame by composing the inter-frame transformations. The 3D stage then uses
the slope geometry reconstructed by the previous modules, rather than estimating a new plane
rectification from the trajectory frames alone.

\medskip

The slope reconstruction provides the camera matrix \(K\), two reconstructed track borders, the
track normal, and a center plane between the two borders. The center plane is represented by its
origin \(\mathbf{O}_c\) and by two basis directions \(\mathbf{e}_1\) and \(\mathbf{e}_2\). Let
\(\mathbf{n}\) be the reconstructed normal between the two track borders, let \(w\) be the track
width used by the reconstruction, and let \(\mathbf{O}_A\) be the first reconstructed point on one
track border. The corresponding point on the parallel border is
\(\mathbf{O}_B=\mathbf{O}_A+w\mathbf{n}\), so the origin of the center plane is
\[
\mathbf{O}_c = \frac{\mathbf{O}_A+\mathbf{O}_B}{2}.
\]
The first basis direction \(\mathbf{e}_1\) is obtained from the direction of the reconstructed track
border, projected onto the plane orthogonal to \(\mathbf{n}\) and normalized. The second basis
direction is then chosen as \(\mathbf{e}_2=\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{e}_1\). From this information the
code builds the homography
\(H_{\text{image}\rightarrow\text{center}}\) that maps image points in the reference frame to
coordinates on the center plane.

\medskip

For a frame \(t\), let \(p_t\) be the measured 2D jumper point in the original image, let
\(H_{t\rightarrow r}\) be the cumulative transform from frame \(t\) to the reference frame \(r\),
and let \(\mathbf{q}_t\) be the corresponding point in center-plane coordinates. The mapping is:
\[
\mathbf{q}_t \sim H_{\text{image}\rightarrow\text{center}}\,H_{t\rightarrow r}\,p_t .
\]
If \(\mathbf{q}_t=(u_t,v_t)\), where \(u_t\) and \(v_t\) are the two coordinates on the center-plane
basis, then the 3D point \(\mathbf{X}_t\) is obtained as:
\[
\mathbf{X}_t = \mathbf{O}_{c} + u_t \mathbf{e}_1 + v_t \mathbf{e}_2 .
\]
Thus, the reported 3D trajectory is the stabilized jumper trajectory lifted onto the reconstructed
center plane of the slope.
