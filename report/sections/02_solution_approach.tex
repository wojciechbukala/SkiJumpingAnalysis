\section{Pipeline overview}
The current solution keeps the first part of the previous 2D pipeline and extends it with the geometric modules needed to obtain a 3D trajectory. The unchanged front end is composed of athlete detection, pixel-level segmentation, temporal filtering, and shot segmentation. These stages still produce a filtered image-space center-of-mass sequence for the jumper. The new part of the pipeline then extracts geometric features, estimates the camera calibration, reconstructs the slope reference geometry, and maps the stabilized jumper trajectory onto that reconstructed geometry,this last step was used also in the last submission.

\medskip

At a high level, the processing chain is:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item detect and track the jumper in the video;
    \item segment the jumper inside the detected bounding box and compute the center of mass;
    \item filter the center-of-mass sequence with a Kalman filter;
    \item split the broadcast video into camera intervals;
    \item extract stable image lines from a selected geometry frame;
    \item estimate the camera intrinsic matrix from vanishing-point constraints;
    \item reconstruct the slope geometry and lift the stabilized jumper trajectory into 3D.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Unchanged 2D front end}
The first four stages are inherited from the previous pipeline. Their role is to provide reliable 2D measurements before any geometric reconstruction is attempted.

\subsection{Athlete detection and ROI extraction}
The video is processed frame by frame with a YOLO-based detector trained for the ski-jumping domain. For each frame, the detector returns one or more bounding boxes for the jumper. When multiple detections are available, the pipeline keeps the highest-confidence detection and uses YOLO tracking to preserve a consistent identity over time. The selected bounding box has two roles: it defines the image region where the jumper will be segmented, and it identifies the moving foreground area that must be masked out during background motion estimation.

\subsection{Pixel-level segmentation and center of mass}
The detected bounding box is passed to the Segment Anything Model. Applying SAM only inside the YOLO region of interest reduces the amount of irrelevant image content and makes the segmentation more stable. The binary mask returned by SAM is used to estimate the image-space center of mass of the jumper. This gives the raw measurement
\[
p_t = (x_t,y_t)
\]
for frame \(t\). The same CSV output stores both the raw center of mass and the filtered coordinates used by the later trajectory stage.

\subsection{Temporal filtering}
The raw center-of-mass measurements can contain frame-to-frame jitter because the visible silhouette changes and segmentation is not perfectly stable. For this reason, the pipeline applies a constant-velocity Kalman filter. The filter predicts the next position from the previous state and then corrects this prediction with the current measurement. The output is a smoother sequence \((kf_x,kf_y)\), which is the sequence used by the geometric stages rather than applying a separate post-processing smoother at the end.

\subsection{Shot segmentation}
Broadcast ski-jumping videos contain several camera cuts. A transformation estimated across a camera cut is not meaningful, so the video is first split into camera intervals. Consecutive frames are downscaled for efficiency, converted to HSV color space, and represented by normalized two-dimensional histograms over hue and saturation. The Bhattacharyya distance between consecutive histograms is used as the cut score. When the distance exceeds the configured threshold and the minimum scene-length constraint is satisfied, the frame is marked as a shot change.

The detected cuts define homogeneous camera intervals. These intervals are later used in two ways: the trajectory stage selects the interval containing the reference frame, and the geometry stages use the semantic interval labels to choose useful frames for inrun and landing analysis.

\section{Feature extraction}
After shot segmentation, the pipeline needs geometric image features that can support camera calibration and slope reconstruction. The feature-extraction stage starts from a selected video frame, typically a frame where the relevant hill geometry is visible and stable. The frame is converted to grayscale, smoothed, and combined with Sobel gradient magnitude. Canny edge detection and a small morphological closing produce an edge map.

The code uses OpenCV's Line Segment Detector; Candidate segments are filtered by length, line width, and edge support, then deduplicated. The result is saved as numbered line previews and a CSV file. If the automatic detector does not provide enough useful lines, the same tool can be run in manual mode, where the user clicks the line endpoints directly.

The purpose of this stage is to create a clean set of image lines that can be grouped according to the real-world directions they represent. Those line groups are the input to the camera-calibration stage.

\section{Camera calibration}
The camera calibration stage estimates the intrinsic matrix \(K\), which is required to convert image points and vanishing points into camera-space rays and directions. The camera is modeled with the pinhole projection equation
\[
\mathbf{x} \sim K [R \mid t] \mathbf{X},
\]
where \(\mathbf{X}\) is a 3D scene point, \(\mathbf{x}\) is its homogeneous image projection, and \([R \mid t]\) is the camera pose. With zero skew, the intrinsic matrix is
\[
K =
\begin{bmatrix}
f_x & 0 & c_x \\
0 & f_y & c_y \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Since no checkerboard calibration sequence is available, calibration is obtained from scene geometry. Lines that are parallel in 3D meet at a common vanishing point in the image. For each selected group of image lines, the corresponding vanishing point is estimated by stacking the homogeneous line equations and solving the least-squares intersection with singular value decomposition.

Let \(\mathbf{v}_i\) and \(\mathbf{v}_j\) be vanishing points associated with two orthogonal 3D directions. The image of the absolute conic is
\[
\omega = K^{-\top}K^{-1},
\]
and orthogonality imposes
\[
\mathbf{v}_i^\top \omega \mathbf{v}_j = 0.
\]
The implementation builds these constraints from the selected vanishing-point pairs and solves for the camera intrinsics. When enabled, equal-length segment constraints provide additional metric information for estimating the principal point. The result is saved as \path{outputGeometry/calibration_K.json} and is reused by both the slope reconstruction and trajectory lifting stages.

\section{Jumper trajectory and slope extraction}
The final part of the pipeline combines the stabilized jumper detections with the reconstructed slope geometry. The jumper-trajectory stage first expresses the athlete measurements in a common reference frame. Slope extraction then provides the 3D reference surface used to lift those stabilized points into space.

\subsection{Jumper trajectory}
The trajectory stage selects the camera interval containing the configured reference frame. Inside this interval, the pipeline reads the Kalman-filtered jumper points from the detection CSV and estimates background motion between consecutive frames. The jumper bounding box and broadcast overlays are masked so that the motion estimate describes the background and camera movement, not the athlete.

The active motion branch is feature based. SIFT keypoints are extracted on the valid background regions, descriptors are matched with L2 distance and Lowe's ratio test, and RANSAC estimates a homography from the current frame to the previous frame. If the homography score is too low, an affine fallback is attempted; if that is also unreliable, the frame pair is assigned an identity transform and a low motion score.

All pairwise transformations are normalized as \(3 \times 3\) homographies and composed toward the selected reference frame \(r\). For frame \(t\), the cumulative transform \(H_{t\rightarrow r}\) maps the filtered image point \(\mathbf{p}_t\) into the reference image:
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_t \sim H_{t\rightarrow r}\mathbf{p}_t.
\]
For frames after the reference, transformations are composed forward along the interval; for frames before the reference, inverse transformations are used. The pairwise confidence scores are propagated along the same chain, and points with cumulative score below the configured threshold are marked as less reliable.

At this point the trajectory is stabilized in the image coordinate system of the reference frame. The remaining step is to build the geometric surface on which these reference-frame points will be placed.

\subsection{Slope extraction and 3D lifting}
Slope extraction starts from the calibrated camera and a frame where the relevant track geometry is visible. The user annotates two types of information: segments that indicate the direction normal to the projected slope plane, and two ordered polylines representing the visible track borders. The annotated normal-direction segments are converted into homogeneous image lines, and their least-squares intersection gives a vanishing point \(\mathbf{v}_n\). The corresponding camera-space normal direction is
\[
\mathbf{n} = \frac{K^{-1}\mathbf{v}_n}{\|K^{-1}\mathbf{v}_n\|}.
\]

The two track-border polylines are resampled into a fixed number of points. For each point on the first border, a line through the normal vanishing point is used to find the corresponding point on the second border. A monotonic arc-length regularization keeps these correspondences ordered along the curves. Each correspondence therefore defines two camera rays, one for each projected border point.

Given a known or unit track width \(w\), the reconstruction solves for two parallel 3D curves separated by \(w\mathbf{n}\). If \(r_a\) and \(r_b\) are the camera rays of a corresponding pair, the depths are estimated so that the reconstructed points satisfy the parallel-plane constraint as closely as possible:
\[
\mathbf{P}_b - \mathbf{P}_a \approx w\mathbf{n}.
\]
The output contains the left and right reconstructed track borders, the centerline
\[
\mathbf{P}_c = \frac{\mathbf{P}_a+\mathbf{P}_b}{2},
\]
the oriented normal, correspondence errors, and a static 3D plot.

The visualization stage then derives a center plane from the reconstructed borders. Let \(\mathbf{O}_A\) be the first point on one border and let \(\mathbf{O}_B=\mathbf{O}_A+w\mathbf{n}\) be the corresponding point on the parallel border. The center-plane origin is
\[
\mathbf{O}_c = \frac{\mathbf{O}_A+\mathbf{O}_B}{2}.
\]
The first plane basis vector \(\mathbf{e}_1\) is obtained from the reconstructed track direction after removing any component along \(\mathbf{n}\), and the second basis vector is \(\mathbf{e}_2=\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{e}_1\). This gives the homography from center-plane coordinates to the image:
\[
H_{\text{center}\rightarrow\text{image}} =
K
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{e}_1 & \mathbf{e}_2 & \mathbf{O}_c
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Its inverse maps image points in the reference frame to coordinates on the reconstructed center plane.

Finally, each stabilized reference-frame trajectory point is lifted onto the reconstructed center plane. Let \(H_{\text{image}\rightarrow\text{center}}\) be the inverse center-plane homography. The plane coordinates of the jumper are
\[
\mathbf{q}_t \sim H_{\text{image}\rightarrow\text{center}}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_t.
\]
If \(\mathbf{q}_t=(u_t,v_t)\), the corresponding 3D point is
\[
\mathbf{X}_t = \mathbf{O}_c + u_t\mathbf{e}_1 + v_t\mathbf{e}_2.
\]
The final output is therefore a stabilized 3D jumper trajectory expressed in the same camera-coordinate reference system as the reconstructed slope.
